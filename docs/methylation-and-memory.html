three kinds of memory

CpG methylation shows up wherever a cell needs to remember something — a synapse that fired, an antigen it fought off, or a body that once went through something hard. Same chemistry (a methyl group on a cytosine before a guanine), three completely different memory systems.

01 — cognitive memory

How a synapse writes something down

For a long time, DNA methylation was treated as a developmental lock — set early, then static for the life of the cell. That view doesn't survive contact with the adult hippocampus. Methylation there is fast and dynamic, added and removed within hours of a learning event.

The workhorse is DNMT3A, the adult brain's dominant de novo methyltransferase. Two isoforms do different jobs: DNMT3A2 behaves like an immediate-early gene, switching on right after neuronal activity and laying down new methyl marks at over a thousand promoters; DNMT3A1 is constitutively present but gets degraded in an activity-dependent way, and silencing it disrupts memory recall. Knocking out DNMT3A (alone or with DNMT1) in forebrain neurons impairs contextual fear memory and spatial memory in mice.

Methylation isn't a one-way ratchet, either. TET enzymes (TET1–3, with TET3 dominant in cortex and hippocampus) oxidize 5-methylcytosine back toward an unmethylated state. A well-studied loop: activity induces a transient double-strand break at the immediate-early gene EGR1's promoter, which recruits TET1 to demethylate and activate hundreds of nearby genes — at the same time DNMT3A2 is methylating a different set. Learning is the two enzyme families pulling in opposite directions at different loci, simultaneously.

Two time scales. Hippocampal methylation looks like a short-lived, working step in memory consolidation — a scratchpad. The more permanent copy is thought to shift to cortex over time, which is one molecular echo of systems-level memory consolidation (the same process behind why very old memories survive hippocampal damage but recent ones don't).

Where this touches your CD55 work

Nanopore gives you native 5mC calls without bisulfite conversion — the exact readout this literature is built on, just usually via bisulfite pyrosequencing or WGBS. A "neurons remember with methylation too" panel is a natural way to widen epicme.bio beyond immune cells without leaving your actual technical wheelhouse.

02 — immune memory

The memory your T cells already have

This is the version closest to home. A naive CD8⁺ T cell that meets its antigen differentiates into an effector cell, and that transition is written into the genome as a broad wave of methylation change. If the antigen clears, a subset becomes a long-lived memory T cell that keeps a "multipotent" epigenetic signature layered under its effector-associated marks — poised to reactivate fast on re-exposure, without needing to relearn its program from scratch.

If the antigen doesn't clear — chronic infection, or a tumor — the same starting cell instead becomes exhausted. And exhaustion has its own, distinct, remarkably stable methylation landscape. The clearest single example is PDCD1 (PD-1): its promoter demethylates during the effector phase in both fates, but only memory cells go back and remethylate it. Exhausted CD8⁺ T cells keep it demethylated and transcriptionally open — even after the virus is undetectable. The mark, not the antigen, becomes the record.

This is why checkpoint blockade alone often only partially rescues exhausted T cells: you can block PD-1 signaling, but you haven't erased the methylation program underneath it. It's also why DNMT3A deletion or DNMT inhibitors (e.g. decitabine) in CAR-T cells is an active engineering strategy — remove the "de novo methylator" and cells stay closer to a memory-like, rejuvenatable state under chronic antigen exposure, rather than sliding into exhaustion.

The same enzyme you're studying in CD55 knockdown — the CRISPRoff/ZFP-off methylation machinery — is mechanistically the same lever T cells pull on themselves during differentiation. Your donor T cells already carry this history in their methylome before you ever touch them.
03 — biological embedding

When an experience becomes a cell

This is the part you asked about directly, and it's the most interesting and the most contested corner of the field. The core claim isn't that trauma "changes your DNA" — sequence is untouched — it's that psychologically significant experience can shift the methylation state of specific stress-regulatory genes, measurably, in blood.

FKBP5 — the stress thermostat

FKBP5 is a co-chaperone that dampens glucocorticoid receptor signaling — it's part of the negative feedback loop that normally shuts a cortisol spike back down. Its intron 7 carries glucocorticoid response elements, and methylation there is allele-dependent: carriers of a particular risk variant (rs1360780 T allele) who also experienced childhood trauma show demethylation at this site, in both peripheral blood and hippocampal progenitor cells — and demethylation means more FKBP5, a weaker brake on the stress response, present decades after the original exposure. Encouragingly, this mark isn't fixed: intervention studies (trauma-focused psychotherapy, an 8-week mindfulness program) have found FKBP5 methylation shifting in step with symptom improvement.

NR3C1 and the maternal-care lineage

The foundational finding here (Meaney's rodent work, later echoed in human postmortem brain from suicide victims with a history of childhood abuse) is early caregiving quality leaving a lasting methylation signature at the glucocorticoid receptor promoter, tuning HPA-axis reactivity for the rest of the animal's — or person's — life.

Reading the blood as a proxy for the brain

The honest caveat researchers keep repeating: tissue matters. Whole-blood methylation is a proxy, not a direct readout of what's happening in cortex or hippocampus. But it isn't a wild guess either — for FKBP5 specifically, methylation at the key intron 7 regulatory region shows meaningfully high correlation between blood and brain tissue (prefrontal cortex, hippocampus), which is a big part of why it's treated as a plausible peripheral biomarker at all.

worth holding onto
Most of this literature is correlational and cross-sectional. Effect sizes at individual CpGs are typically small; findings often don't replicate cleanly across cohorts; and causal direction is genuinely hard to pin down — does trauma cause the methylation change, does baseline methylation shape trauma vulnerability, or both, mediated by something else (genotype, prior stress, current symptoms)? "Biological embedding" is a real and active research program, not settled fact at the level of any single gene.
connecting thread

Same mark, same logic

All three systems reuse the identical trick: make a transient state durable by writing it into methylation, at a specific locus, in a specific cell type. A synapse's momentary activity becomes a promoter's stable methyl mark. An antigen encounter becomes PD-1's locked-open (or locked-closed) state. A period of chronic stress becomes FKBP5's demethylated intron. None of these are the genome being rewritten — all are the same molecule, cytosine plus a methyl group at a CpG, functioning as the cell's way of keeping a diary.

The open question worth sitting with for epicme.bio: what makes a mark reversible (hippocampal consolidation, FKBP5 under therapy) versus "locked" (exhausted-T-cell PD-1)? That's not just a neat parallel — it's a real mechanistic question about chromatin stability that your own Nanopore methylation data on CD55 editing durability could actually speak to.
